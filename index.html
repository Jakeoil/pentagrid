Pentagrid

Explorations of de Bruijn's method for Penrose tilings

Take five families of equally spaced parallel lines, turned 72° from one another, each shifted by an offset γj. The lines cut the plane into regions. Give every region the five integers

Kj(x) = ⌈ x · vj + γj ⌉

and send each region to the point f (x) = ∑ Kj vj. What comes out is a Penrose rhomb tiling — every one of them, as γ varies. That is de Bruijn's 1981 result, and this site is an attempt to make it something you can put your hands on rather than read.

From grid to tiling

Every region between the lines has a fixed tuple of five integers Kj, one per family, counting how many lines of that family you have crossed. Send each region to f = ∑ Kj vj and adjacent regions land one vj apart. The four regions meeting at a crossing therefore land on the four corners of a rhomb — so each crossing on the left is one tile on the right, fat if the two families are adjacent, thin if they are two apart.

Three things worth knowing before you touch the sliders

  • Regularity is decided, not guessed. The construction needs no three lines concurrent. That turns out to have a closed form: a triple is singular exactly when one particular γ is an integer and a particular pair of them sums to an integer. So if no γj is an integer, the pentagrid is regular everywhere — ten integer comparisons, no tolerance. The keep γ regular checkbox holds you off the singular set; the default γ = 0 is on it, with all five lines through the origin.
  • Most regions are far too small to click. Not as an edge case — an ordinary γ already has regions of a few hundredths of a pixel, because the near-concurrent triples equidistribute. The meter counts them and the loupe opens automatically when you approach one, magnifying as far as it takes. Move into the panel to hover inside it.
  • The tiling is 2½ times the grid that makes it. The dual map has gain 5/2, so the two pictures do not line up by default. It is not an accident: the construction projects ℤ5 onto a plane, each basis vector keeps 2/5 of its length there, and 5/2 is the reciprocal. The register 5:2 toggle puts every rhomb back on the crossing that generated it.

Explorations

Each is a project of its own rather than another step in the walkthrough, with its own page.

The Wieringa roof →
Give every vertex a height of half its de Bruijn index and the flat tiling stands up. Both rhombs become the same golden rhombus — edge √5/2, diagonals φ:1 — every edge rises or falls by exactly a half, and the whole roof sits on four levels. Fold it flat and back, and grow it from the pentagrid in three dimensions.
Growing the tiling →
Start with the pentagrid and a dot on every crossing, then grow the dots into tiles. They cannot all grow in place, so they slide apart — and the grid lines come with them, each staying fastened to the edge midpoints it passes between, until the five straight families have been dragged into the wandering ribbons of the finished tiling.
Wiggle room →
Five offsets, minus one for the sum, leaves four — but two of those do nothing at all, because shifting γ along the directions slides the pentagrid and leaves the tiling exactly where it was. So the pattern turns on two numbers, and the γ that preserve a given patch form a convex region you can draw, drag around inside, and measure.
  • The discrete pentagrid planned. Replace the 72° directions with the limiting directions of an integer Penrose construction, 34.6438° and 71.1377°, and dualise that instead. Whether the result is still two rhomb shapes is the first thing to find out.
