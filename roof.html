The Wieringa roof

The same tiling, folded into golden rhombi

The height was in the data all along. Give every vertex a z of half its de Bruijn index — half the sum of its five Kj — and the flat tiling stands up:

Ej = vj + ½ž   ⇒   z = (∑ Kj) / 2

Every face becomes the same golden rhombus — edge √5 / 2, diagonals in ratio φ, angles 63.43° and 116.57°. Thick and thin stop being different shapes and become the same shape lying at different tilts; which one you see depends only on which corner sits at the shared vertex. Every edge rises or falls by exactly ½.

How many levels the roof stands on is a property of the offsets rather than of the construction. With Σγ = 0 the index runs 1 – 4 and the roof has four; move the sum and the range shifts, sometimes to five. The golden rhombus does not care — each face is spanned by two of the Ej, which know nothing about γ — so the relief changes and the shape never does.

Drag to pan, right-drag to spin and tilt, scroll to zoom. fold at 0 lays the roof flat — the ordinary tiling — and grow still runs the whole assembly from the pentagrid, now in three dimensions.

The singularities are solids

Where k grid lines meet at a point, the tiling has no room for the C(k,2) rhombs that crossing makes, and they pile up on the spot. Flat, that is a defect. Folded, it is not: the regions around the point are the 2k corners of a k-cube, and because z counts the chosen generators, the cube's own main diagonal stands up as the vertical. The pile becomes a zonohedron made of the roof's own golden rhombus — three lines a golden rhombohedron, four Bilinski's rhombic dodecahedron, five the rhombic icosahedron. Turn on solids and they grow out of their crossings with everything else.

A solid seen in outline has more than one reading, the way a Necker cube does, and each reading is one rhombic tiling of the 2k-gon. The way to count them is to get rid of the singularity: nudge each of the k lines a little, up or down, and every pair crosses at a point of its own, which lays the rhombs out in a definite order. Every reading is a configuration of the lines in this sense — 2 for the hexagon, 8 for the octagon, 62 for the decagon — so nothing on the slider is an abstraction over the grid. reading steps through them, least disturbance first, and the readout names the configuration: 0↑ 1↓↓ 2· 3↑ 4↓, a double arrow for twice the step and a dot for a line left where it was.

One equal step each way — the plainest nudge there is — accounts for the hexagon and the octagon completely, but only 32 of the decagon's 62. Letting a line stay put reaches 42; the last twenty need one line moved twice as far as another. And the readings do not merely use the solid's surface: a reading cuts through its interior, so each of the C(k,2) face directions turns up at all 2k−2 of its positions — 80 distinct rhombs for the decagon, against the 20 on the hull. Every face of the solid is in play.

From a given reading only some of its hexagons can be turned over — three or four, and exactly five at the two extremes. flip chooses which, pair draws that one turned as well, and since only a single cell's cap differs, what you see is three rhombs against three with the raised cap in front. That flip is the phason flip.

The decagon keeps one secret the plane cannot show. At Σγ = 0 the five directions sum to zero, so the icosahedron's bottom and top corners cast the same shadow — 32 corners in space, 31 in the plane. The ghost center that carries index 0 and 5 at once is simply the far pole, and tilting the roof pulls the two apart.

Because z is affine in a tile's own coordinates, each face stays exactly planar and the surface is a height field, so the faces can simply be sorted by depth: no 3D library, and the ordering is exact. The ribbons run over the roof rather than through it, following the same edge midpoints they did in the plane — which is what turns the five flat pencils of grid lines into strips lying across a folded surface.
