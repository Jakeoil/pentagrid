E2 — the discrete wheel

Where the integer substitution actually points

The wheel is ten lattice points: three free seeds and their reflections. Inflation replaces each seed with the sum of three consecutive entries, and because the two reflections act on the two coordinates differently, x and y evolve under two different integer matrices. That asymmetry is the whole story — a similarity would use one matrix for both.

        ⎡1 0 0⎤              ⎡1 2 0⎤
  Mx =  ⎢1 1 1⎥        My =  ⎢1 1 1⎥
        ⎣0 1 2⎦              ⎣0 1 0⎦

  char(Mx) = (1−λ)(λ²−3λ+1)      char(My) = (1+λ)(λ²−3λ+1)

Both carry φ² and φ⁻², so the growth ratio is φ² — proved, not measured. Mx carries an extra eigenvalue 1: x₀ never moves, which is why the first seed sits on the vertical at every generation. My carries an extra −1: an alternating component that neither grows nor decays.

Where this leaves a multigrid. Five directions, unequally spaced, mirror symmetric about the vertical. Every step of de Bruijn's construction that reads the direction array still works — crossings, K-tuples, the dual map. What breaks is everything that assumed the spacing was even:

  • The rhomb class min(k−j, n−(k−j)) names a shape only when the families are evenly spread. Here the ten family pairs could give ten parallelogram types rather than two golden rhombs — and whether they collapse to fewer is the first result.
  • The registration gain n/2 comes from ∑vvᵀ = (n/2)I, which needs a tight frame. The discrete frame is diagonal — the mirror symmetry survives — but not isotropic, so the dual map is a linear map with two different gains rather than a similarity.
  • ∑vj ≠ 0, so ∑K is not a height function: no Wieringa lift, no index argument, no four levels.

Nothing above is a conclusion about the dual tiling. It is the list of what has to be rebuilt before there is one.
