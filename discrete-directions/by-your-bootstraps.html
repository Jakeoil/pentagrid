By your bootstraps

Generate a whole Penrose tiling from a single tile

penrose-mosaic holds four wheels and eight tile outlines, every one of them found by hand on quadrille paper in the 1990s and typed in as coordinates. This page keeps five ordered lattice points — the corners of one pentagon — and derives the rest. Drag a corner and the tiling follows, because nothing downstream holds a coordinate of its own.

Left: the five points, draggable, with the controls that make them and the patch. Right: the tiling they generate. Scroll either canvas to zoom, drag the paper to pan, double-click the paper to put both back and fit, and double-click a handle to make it the floater. Extrapolate walks the pentagon up and down the wheel ladder in half generations — 0 is the five themselves — and use adopts whatever is shown as the new five. Everything below is how it works.

The five points are the pentagon pentaUp, and they are ordered. That is the whole lever: the order is the rotation operator, so a rotation is an index shift and nothing else. There is no reflection anywhere in the derivation — no vr, no hr, no table of stored rotations — which is what lets the five points be asymmetric. Move one and the figure stops being mirror symmetric; everything still works.

Step 1 — the pentaflake, and measuring P off it

The wheels are not given. They are measured off a figure you build, and this is the figure. Jake's construction:

Copy 1 2 3 4 5. Rotate the copy 180° through O. Put it so that edge 12 touches 21; the next copy so 23 touches 32, then 34–43, 45–54, and 51 touches 15. That is a pentaflake. All the copies are yellow. The first value of your P wheel (gen 1) will be O to O1, O to O2, etc.

Rotating 180° through O sends corner j to −pts[j]. For the copy's edge (k+1 → k) to land on the original's edge (k → k+1), the copy must move by T, and both corners give the same T — which is what makes the edges coincide rather than merely abut:

  −pts[k+1] + T = pts[k]        and        −pts[k] + T = pts[k+1]

  so   O_k  =  pts[k] + pts[k+1]

A P vector is the two corners of the shared edge, added. That is the measurement, and it needs no wheel, no formula and no reflection — only that the list is ordered.

The five leaf centers land on the odd tenths of P — the down half — because a leaf is the 180° copy and faces the other way. The even tenths are the same construction run on a leaf, which is the flake seen from one of its own petals. Ten directions, one figure.

And this is why P[t] = D[t−1] + D[t+1] holds: at an odd tenth, that stride-1 pair is the two corners of a shared edge. The formula is the pentaflake written down, not an algebraic coincidence.

The other preset: a regular pentagon

Nothing here needs the lattice. The wheels, the walks, the substitution and the rhombs are arithmetic on pairs of numbers, and the integers are only what the quadrille happens to use. So the second preset is the regular pentagon, centered on the origin with side 4 — the normalization that makes it comparable, since the quadrille's three edge lengths are 4, √13 and √17 and this is what they are approximating. Its circumradius is 4 / (2 sin 36°) = 3.402603, against the quadrille's 3.

  quadrille  (0,-3)      (3,-1)      (2,3)      (-2,3)      (-3,-1)
  real       (0,-3.403)  (3.236,-1.051)  (2,2.753)  (-2,2.753)  (-3.236,-1.051)

  sides      quadrille  3.606  4.123  4.000  4.123  3.606
             real       4.000  4.000  4.000  4.000  4.000

Fed through the same code it lands on Euclidean Penrose exactly and at once — thick 72°, thin 36°, at every generation, with no convergence at all, because a regular pentagon is already the substitution's fixed point. The quadrille has to climb toward 69.287628° and 37.724519° and never reaches 72°. The edge wheel tells the same story in one number: the real pentagon has one edge length, the quadrille three.

The presets are a button each rather than a dropdown — the choices are visible, switching is one press, and custom stops having to be a fake entry in the list: it is simply no button pressed in. The button pressed in is the preset the five currently are, compared on the spokes, so sliding a pentagon away from its origin releases it. reset reloads whichever one you last chose.

Snap is on for the quadrille and off for the real preset — rounding an irrational pentagon's corners onto the paper on the first drag would silently turn it back into a quadrille. The box follows the figure and is yours to override either way. With it on, each handle lands on the grid it belongs to: the five corners on whole units, because they are Gaussian integers and that is the definition, and the center on fifths, because the sweet spot is always in (1/5)ℤ² and a whole-unit center could never reach it. One setting, two grids, because the two handles are not the same kind of thing.

Fine is the center's grid made visible: the five lighter lines across each unit square, drawn under the unit lines rather than over them, since the corners sit on the unit vertices. It is off by default, because close in on a corner it is a thicket over the only grid that corner can land on. But it comes up by itself, grayed, whenever the center is somewhere the unit lattice cannot describe — off a whole unit, or being dragged off one — and goes back down, to whatever you last set it to, when the center returns to a unit. A grayed check means there is nothing for a click to do.

When the center reaches the centroid it becomes a bullseye: a white pupil inside the dot, and on the sweet spot in the readout. Nothing about the outline announces it, and it is the state where P = S, inflate(D) = T and all three rhomb spellings close as one exact parallelogram.

Two centers, and you have to choose

The centroid row is that choice, as two boxes. Each one is half question and half instruction: it reads checked and grayed when the center is already there, and clicking it when it is not takes the center there in one press.

  • actual is the centroid itself — §5's sweet spot, where every rhomb closes as an exact parallelogram. It is in (1/5)ℤ², so it costs you an origin that is not a lattice point: the spokes stop being Gaussian integers and every wheel derived from them comes off the unit lattice onto the fifths. That is the whole of §9's complaint about the classic rhombs.
  • closest is that point rounded to the nearest whole unit. The spokes stay integers and every wheel above stays on the lattice, and you pay for it in exactness: the center is up to (½, ½) off the centroid, and §5's parallelograms are off by that much. It turns fine back off on the way, since getting the center onto a unit is the point of pressing it.

On an even pentad there is nothing to choose: the five sum to a multiple of five, the centroid is already a lattice point, and both boxes come up grayed and checked at once. That is the quickest test on the page for whether a seed is even — load the even preset and look at the row.

The floater is one handle driven by the others. Double-click a handle to assign it, double-click it again to release it. What it holds is Σ spokes = 5·(centroid − origin) — the origin's offset from the centroid — at whatever value that had when you assigned it.

By default the center floats, so a corner drag carries the origin with it: pull a point by δ and the origin follows by δ/5, keeping its relationship to the figure instead of being left behind by it. Check centroid actual first and the held value is zero, and then no drag can leave the sweet spot — P = S, inflate(D) = T, all three rhomb spellings one exact parallelogram, however you reshape the pentagon. Choose one of the five instead and the center is fixed: it draws small, the size it already uses off the base rung, because it is not a handle any more. Now the centroid is pinned and the pentagon flexes about it — drag point 1 by δ and the floating point answers with −δ. Dragging the floater itself is how you retarget; it is the sum strip, not the locked dial. A driven handle is never snapped, because it is determined rather than chosen, and with the five on units it generally lands on a fifth — which is the grid the center snaps to anyway. This is the Reticulum's locked γ in this geometry, and the same arithmetic: moveHandle in src/math/seed.js re-derives the dependent handle after every move, and nothing in the interface recomputes any of it.

The third preset: asymmetric

Both presets above are symmetric — the quadrille has a vertical mirror, the regular pentagon has five — so neither of them can show the claim this page is really making: that no step here uses a reflection. The odd tenths are negations of the even ones, which needs no mirror axis, so five points with no symmetry at all go through the wheels, the walks, the substitution and the rhombs unchanged. The asymmetric preset is a pentagon with no mirror on any axis:

  point        0        1       2       3       4
              (-2,-4)  (3,-3)  (3,2)  (-1,4)  (-5,0)

  bearing      333.4°   45.0°  123.7°  194.0°  270.0°   clockwise from up
  gap           71.6°   78.7°   70.3°   76.0°   63.4°   ideal 72°
  radius         4.47     4.24    3.61    4.12    5.00

Visibly lopsided, and it tiles. Over five generations not one pentagon, star, boat or diamond outline crosses itself, and the tiles still meet — the test that checks this now runs on four seeds, and this is the one that would catch an outline quietly leaning on a symmetry the other three happen to have.

Not every five points will do, which is worth knowing before you drag. What a seed has to satisfy is a condition on the ten spokes, not on the five points: the five and their negatives must interleave, each step forward by more than nothing and less than a half turn, closing at exactly one full turn. A perfectly convex-looking pentagon fails it if its five bearings wind twice round instead of once, or if any two of the five are parallel or antiparallel — that aims two of the ten spokes the same way, and no relabeling of the points can separate them.

The fourth preset: even

The three above were drawn. This one was searched for, and it is better than the quadrille at the two things the quadrille is bad at:

  point        0       1       2        3       4
               (3,2)   (0,2)  (-3,2)  (-2,-3)  (2,-3)       sum (0,0)

  radius        3.606   2.000   3.606    3.606   3.606

Its five points sum to zero, so its centroid — the sweet spot, where the rhombs close as exact parallelograms — is the lattice point (0, 0) rather than a fifth. The quadrille's is (0, ⅕), which is why putting it on the sweet spot takes the whole figure off the lattice, and why scaling the quadrille by five to fix that makes everything five times larger. This one needs no scaling: both centroid boxes come up checked and nothing has to move at all.

And the property survives the ladder. The sum lives in one Fourier mode of the wheel, and a generation multiplies that mode by −1, so a sum of zero stays zero at every rung up and down. It is not something you arrange once and lose at the next step.

It also settles twelve times closer to Penrose. What survives inflation besides the figure is one ratio — the counter-rotating part of the decagon against the rotating part — and no generation washes it out. The quadrille's is 2.51%, this one's is 0.20%, which is rhombs within 0.37% of equilateral and a fifth of a degree of 72° and 36°, against the quadrille's 69.29–74.22°. At generation 0 it looks worse than the quadrille — one spoke of 2 among four of √13 — because that lopsidedness is a different mode, and that mode dies. GEOMETRY.md §8 has the modes, and tools/pentads.mjs is the search.

The output wears the same paper, and the two checkboxes under grid say how. 5 space divides its unit squares into fifths, as the input's are, so the two canvases go on reading as one sheet; it is a choice rather than always on, because the output's lattice is where the tiles live and a tile corner is either on a lattice point or it is not — the fifths are the center's business, not theirs. The input has its own, called fine, under grid — and that one manages itself, as above. On top draws that lattice over the tiles instead of only under them, which is the only way to see whether a corner landed on a lattice point, and it has a palette of its own. The pale blues are made to be read through on white paper; over a blue tile, which is the same hue at almost the same value, they were not there at all. The over-grid is teal and deliberately not black — tile edges are black, and a lattice line you cannot tell from an outline is worse than no lattice line. Over the tiles there is no room left to darken into, so the emphasis every 4 units is carried by a thicker stroke rather than a darker one, and the fifths stay the faintest thing on the sheet.

Each canvas styles itself. The input figure's face and edges govern the input figure and nothing else; they are folded away under settings, since they are set once and then in the way of the handles. The output has its own pair, penta face and penta edges, between gen and rhombs. One pair used to govern both, on the reasoning that a pentagon is a face like any other — but the two figures are doing different jobs. The input is a thing you are placing on a lattice, so it starts transparent and the fifths, the unit lines and the fence read straight through it. The output is a tiling, where the fills are how you tell a Pe5 from a Pe3, so it starts solid. Wanting one of them see-through was never an argument about the other.

The seed is in one place or the other. The ▾ pentadiculum button above the rig is the toggle: the instrument is either in the left column, where it starts, or floating as a panel at the output's top-right corner. When it floats, the input canvas and its own controls go with it and the output's controls stay where they are, on the left of the output. Drag the panel by its bar, fold it to just the bar with ▾, resize it from the grip in its bottom-right corner, and close it with × — closing it is the same as pressing the toggle, and puts it back in the column.

The panel is a complete instrument — the same preset, extrapolate, grid, centroid and display rows, and the same settings fold for face and edges — under the same names, so flipping between the two is not a translation. There is only ever one seed and one set of settings: whichever row you touched is the one that is right, and the other follows. Where you leave the panel, how wide you made it and whether you folded it are remembered between visits.

The bar names the seed, not the panel: pentadiculum — even, or — custom once you have dragged it off a preset. Folded, it adds the origin and sweet when the center is on the sweet spot, so folding it to a bar costs you nothing you were reading. The one thing the panel does not carry is the input figure's face and edges — those are how this page draws the seed rather than anything about the seed, they keep whatever you last set, and the settings fold that sets them comes back with the column.

Both canvases zoom and both pan, and smoothly: the cell is whatever the fit and the wheel make it, with no ladder of allowed sizes. Scroll over either one to zoom; drag where there is nothing to grab to pan. On the input that means anywhere off the six handles — drag a handle and you move the seed, drag the paper and you move the view — and a double-click on empty paper puts the zoom, the pan and the fit all back at once, while a double-click on a handle assigns the floater. The target is the dot you can see, measured in pixels against the radius it is drawn at. It used to be two and a half lattice units, which is a different size on every seed and at every zoom: most of the even pentad at rest, and the whole canvas once you had zoomed in to place a corner, so every press landed on a handle and the paper could not be panned at all. A handle you are holding goes translucent, so the lattice point you are placing it on is not hidden by the thing you are placing. The pan is kept in lattice units rather than pixels, so zooming in after panning keeps what you panned to in the middle instead of throwing it off the edge. The zoom is about the middle of the canvas and not the pointer, which is what makes that work. Its automatic fit holds still while you drag and gives way only when a corner reaches the frame, a percent a frame. It used to refit exactly on every frame, which fed back through the pointer and made the figure shoot away from the hand dragging it; src/ui/fit.js has the loop written out.

The ladder, in half steps and below zero

A generation is φ² — two Penrose deflations — so the natural index is the half. Turn the slider on the input figure and the pentagon walks it:

  gen   D seeds (tenths 0, 1, 2)      |D[1]|
  -1    (0,1)   (1,-1)   (0,-1)      1.414
 -0.5   (0,-2)  (0,0)    (1,0)       0.000   ← a seed on the origin
    0   (0,-1)  (1,-1)   (1,-1)      1.414
  0.5   (0,-2)  (1,-2)   (2,0)       2.236   ← last rung with every coordinate positive
    1   (0,-3)  (2,-3)   (3,-1)      3.606   ← the five points
  1.5   (0,-6)  (3,-4)   (5,-2)      5.000   ← which is the P wheel at generation 1
    2   (0,-9)  (5,-7)   (8,-3)      8.602
  2.5   (0,-14) (8,-12) (13,-4)     14.422

So the half rung above D is P, exactly — the four wheels were always three consecutive half rungs of one ladder, and naming them D, P and T is what hid it. Whole rungs are reached by inflating or deflating from the seed and half rungs by one φ step from the whole rung below; never by composing half steps, because two φ steps are not one generation. They miss by the alternating (0, ±1) that is My's λ = −1, and the error would pile up. That gap is the same one that makes T and the next generation's D two different integer wheels on one rung.

Downward the ladder is two-sided but not symmetric. Generation ½ is the last rung that reads as a figure. At −½ a seed collapses onto the origin — the deflation running out of wheel, not a numerical accident — and below that the conjugate root ψ = −1/φ takes over, the coordinates alternate in sign and the pentagon turns inside out. Every rung stays exactly on the lattice; it just stops being a pentagon.

What comes out of the five

Three unit vectors a turn apart sum to (1 + 2cos turn) times the middle one, and on a ten-wheel the turn is the stride. So one operator, W[t−k] + W[t] + W[t+k], gives the whole ladder:

stridefactorwhat it is
11 + 2cos 36° = φ²inflate — one generation
21 + 2cos 72° = φthe S wheel
31 + 2cos 108° = φ⁻²deflate — exactly inverse to stride 1
41 + 2cos 144° = −φ⁻¹the conjugate root, ψ

Drop the middle term of the stride-1 sum and 2cos 36° = φ gives P; difference the same pair instead of summing it and you get E, the tile edges — which for the quadrille seed come out as the (4, 0), (3, 2), (1, 4) basis the whole construction was doodled from. T is S + D. Four hand-built arrays, one line each, and every one of them checked against penrose-mosaic's own numbers at generations 0, 1 and 2.

Tiles are closed walks

A tile outline is a list of tenths in E, not a list of coordinates. Rotating it is adding to every entry. That is why the eight stored arrays collapse to four:

  penta     1 3 5 7 9          star      2 0 4 2 6 4 8 6 0 8
  boat      2 0 4 5 6 0 8      diamond   2 3 7 8

  boatWon = turn(boat, 1)      diamondWon = turn(diamond, 1)
  boatToo = turn(boat, 2)      diamondToo = turn(diamond, 2)

The shift is exact in the index. It is not exact in the metric: boatWon has a √13 edge where boatUp has a 4, because the wheel's ten spokes are not the same length. That is the quadrille's character in one line — the combinatorics are exact and the metric is whatever the lattice can manage at that angle.

The rhombs, and a spelling that matters

The P3 overlay walks in the wheels rather than in E. penrose-mosaic builds it as three steps from a corner, the fourth closing — in real geometry every step is a T vector. In quadrille its first step is spelled P + D instead, which is inflate(D): also ×φ², but the other integer representative of that rung. The two differ by the alternating (0, ±1) that is My's λ = −1 eigenvalue.

Because W[k+5] = −W[k], a figure spelled in one wheel has its first and third steps exactly opposite and its closing edge exactly opposite its second — an exact parallelogram, for any five points, and a true rhomb wherever the wheel's spokes agree. Mixing the two spellings loses even that:

  real, all four on T              1.000 1.000 1.000 1.000   rhomb
  quadrille, all four on T         9.434 9.434 9.434 9.434   rhomb at this tenth
  quadrille, all four on D + P     8.602 8.602 8.602 8.602   rhomb at this tenth
  quadrille, penrose-mosaic's mix  8.602 9.434 9.434 8.602   kite

The overlay switch above draws all three. penrose-mosaic's own source flags the line — the comment above goThickReal wonders whether the modes can be unified “if the real of d + p == t”. They cannot, and this is what the difference does. Which spelling the tiling actually wants is not settled here; the kite may be registering against the P1 pentagons deliberately.

Generations, and the diamond space

A single tile is gen 1; the pentaflake above is a Pe5 gen 2. A figure's generation is its parts' plus one, which agrees with the wheel numbering — the seed at index 1. Measured:

  Pe5 gen 2  =  1 Pe5 + 5 Pe3                        the pentaflake
  Pe3 gen 2  =  1 Pe5 + 3 Pe3 + 2 Pe1 + 1 St1        three yellow, two orange
  Pe1 gen 2  =  1 Pe5 + 1 Pe3 + 4 Pe1 + 2 St1
  St1 gen 2  =  1 St5 + 1 Pe1 + 1 St3

A Pe3 gen 2 is the smallest figure with a diamond-shaped hole in it, between its two orange pentagons. Place the tiles and the hole is (0, 3) (1, 7) (0, 11) (−1, 7) — and the St1 the substitution puts there is that polygon exactly. The space and the tile coincide, so the diamond can be measured rather than transcribed.

All four of its sides are √17: it is a genuine rhombus, and a rhombus has a symmetry that swaps its two tips. So the outline alone cannot say which tip carries the tile's reference point — the tips really do look alike, and looking harder will not help. What is true at every diamond tested, on a skew seed as well, is that the reference lies on the long diagonal splitting it

  |p[gen−1]| : |p[gen]|      2 : 6 at generation 2,  →  1 : φ²

— never the midpoint and never a corner. Counting how many pentagons meet at each tip does not settle it: right 40 times out of 40 on a Pe5 patch, and a coin toss (60/60) on a Star patch. The information is not local to the diamond; it comes from whichever parent placed it. Whether a local rule exists at all is open.

Big rhombs, small rhombs, and the rung between

Big and small rhombs are one whole generation apart — φ², two Penrose deflations — which is what goThick(wheels, exp) at exp 0 and 1 means. With the ladder in halves, the rung between them is reachable too: the Robinson level the φ² construction skips. There is a genuine rhomb at every φ step. The rhomb rung slider above offsets the overlay from the tiles it sits on.

The low rungs give out, and not together. At generation 0 the thick rhomb is a square; at generation ½ the thin one has collapsed to a straight line, 180° and 0°, with no interior at all. So the thin's floor sits a half rung above the thick's — the same lopsidedness the wheel ladder has on the way down.

And the angles do not converge on Penrose's. Carried out far enough they settle, to six decimals, on

  thick acute  →  69.287628°   =  2 × 34.643814°
  thin  acute  →  37.724519°   =  180° − 2 × 71.137740°

  Euclidean P3 would be 72° and 36°.

34.643814° and 71.137740° are exactly this construction's own limiting directions. Both rhomb angles are fixed by those two, as they must be since a rhomb's edges are wheel vectors — but it is the first time that has been measured on the tiles rather than on the directions. The quadrille is not a rational approximant creeping toward Penrose. Its rhombs settle on their own angles and stay there.

What is constructed, and what is still borrowed

Constructed: P, off the pentaflake. The pentagon outline, as D[t], D[t+2], D[t+4], D[t+6], D[t+8] — the D wheel at stride 2 about its center, which needs no stored walk and gives exactly two polygons over the ten tenths, the five points and their negatives. The anchors, which are D[t] for a pentagon and P[t] for the whole star family. And the diamond space, as the measured gap.

Still borrowed: the star, boat and diamond walks, and the twist/diamond substitution lists saying how a Pe3 breaks into its children — now the largest transcribed thing in the module. S and T are verified identities but have no figure behind them the way P does; until they do they are description rather than derivation. See NOTES.

Everything drawn here is checked against penrose-mosaic in tests/ — the wheels at three generations, all eight stored tile arrays, the rhomb spellings, and the 55 and 35 rhomb counts of the Sun and Star patches.
