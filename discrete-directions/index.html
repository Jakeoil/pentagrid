Discrete directions

A second geometry, and what happens if you dualize it

Everything in the parent project is de Bruijn's: n families of parallel lines at exactly 2π/n, and a dual tiling in the corresponding cyclotomic field. This corner is about a different object. penrose-mosaic builds Penrose tilings on the integer lattice — every vertex has exact integer coordinates at every generation, and inflation is an integer substitution. That mode is called quadrille, and it is drawn here on the paper it belongs to.

The natural assumption is that such a construction is a rational approximant: that as it inflates its directions converge to the Euclidean 36° and 72°. They do not. The substitution has its own dominant eigenvector, and its limiting slopes are algebraic of degree 2 over ℚ, in ℚ(√5) — a simpler field than the true tangents, which need nested radicals. Two geometries, not one rounded off.

The wheel on show is the P wheel. Writing r for a pentagon's minor radius — its inradius, center to edge — and R for the major one, center to vertex: P is 2r, the vector from one pentagon's center to its neighbor's, which is what the tiling is laid out on. D is R, one pentagon's own radius, center to vertex. The picker carries all four penrose-mosaic stores. S is a pentagon's center to the near diamond's, T two star centers with their feet touching. The figure on the right draws the thing being measured: for P a pentaflake, one pentagon and the five that share its edges, spokes running center to center; for S and T the same pentagon with the spokes reaching further out, to where that other figure sits; for D a single pentagon with its five spokes running center to corner. Which wheel you take sets the scale and nothing else — they share the substitution, so they share its dominant eigenvector and land on the same limiting directions. Their ratio tends to 2r/R = 2 cos 36°  = φ, though the seeds are coarse enough at generation 0 to read 1.387 rather than 1.618.

Whole generations and the half steps between them; past the end, the limit. Scrolling over either figure steps the ladder too. Generation 1 is the seed, as penrose-mosaic numbers it.

seed wheel, degrees from the vertical gaps frame operator

The wheel is ten lattice points, but only the first three are ever stored — the other seven are those three reflected (negate x, negate y, negate both), so they carry nothing new. Three free seeds, and the reflections fill the turn.

Inflation replaces each seed with the sum of three consecutive entries, and because the two reflections act on the two coordinates differently, x and y evolve under two different integer matrices. That asymmetry is the whole story — a similarity would use one matrix for both.

        ⎡1 0 0⎤              ⎡1 2 0⎤
  Mx =  ⎢1 1 1⎥        My =  ⎢1 1 1⎥
        ⎣0 1 2⎦              ⎣0 1 0⎦

  char(Mx) = (1−λ)(λ²−3λ+1)      char(My) = (1+λ)(λ²−3λ+1)

Both carry φ² and φ⁻², so the growth ratio is φ² — proved, not measured. Mx carries an extra eigenvalue 1: x₀ never moves, which is why the first seed sits on the vertical at every generation. The numbering is penrose-mosaic's. Its makeWheels puts the seed at index 1 and one deflation of it at index 0, so that the wheel index matches the shape generation the drawing code asks for — wheels.p[gen] — and that is what measurements.html prints. Generation 1 is the seed here too.

The φ² ladder, and its missing halves. The stored wheels hold only even powers of φ, so their x-components are alternate Fibonacci numbers — 3, 8, 21, 55. The half step is a two-term sum instead of a three-term one (drop the middle), and interleaved the sequence is Fibonacci exactly: 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34, 55, 89. It is a half step on x term for term, and on y up to that alternating ±2 — which is why φ² is where the two coordinates agree. Below zero there is one more positive half generation, then the conjugate ψ = −1/φ takes over. See NOTES.

E2 — dualizing these directions

Five directions, unequally spaced, mirror symmetric about the vertical. Every step of de Bruijn's construction that reads the direction array still works — crossings, K-tuples, the dual map. What breaks is everything that assumed the spacing was even:

  • The rhomb class min(k−j, n−(k−j)) names a shape only when the families are evenly spread. Here the ten family pairs could give ten parallelogram types rather than two golden rhombs — and whether they collapse to fewer is the first result.
  • The registration gain n/2 comes from ∑vvᵀ = (n/2)I, which needs a tight frame. The discrete frame is diagonal — the mirror symmetry survives — but not isotropic, so the dual map is a linear map with two gains rather than a similarity.
  • ∑vj ≠ 0, so ∑K is not a height function: no Wieringa roof, no index argument, no four levels.
  • At any finite generation the slopes are rational, so a grid built from them is periodic — only the limit is irrational. Which generation you dualize is part of the question.

None of that is a conclusion about the dual tiling. It is the list of what has to be rebuilt before there is one. Not started.
