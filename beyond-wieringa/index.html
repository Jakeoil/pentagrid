Beyond Wieringa

The golden zonohedra, as one construction

The Wieringa roof stands a flat tiling up and both rhombs become the same golden rhombus. That is where this starts and not where it stops. Dietl and Eschenburg prove that the five golden isozonohedra — Coxeter's list, every face a golden rhomb — are all projections of cubes from six-dimensional space: K30 = π(C6), F20 = π(C5), B12 = π(C4), and the two rhombohedra at k = 3. The golden rhomb itself is π(C2).

So it is not five solids and, separately, a face. It is one operation with k turned up, and every rung is the face of the one above. Move the slider.

The vertex counts are not measured but forced. Whether a subset of the generators lands on the hull is a question about directions: the sum over T is a vertex exactly when some c is positive on T and negative off it, so vertices correspond to the open regions of an arrangement of k great circles — and such an arrangement has k2 − k + 2 of them. That is 8, 14, 22, 32. The faces are k(k−1), and Euler holds at every rung.

Exploding pulls the faces off, each along its own outward normal, so zero reassembles exactly. Thirty rhombs come off a triacontahedron, twenty off an icosahedron, six off a rhombohedron — and they are all the same rhomb, which is the k = 2 rung seen from the solids above it. Faces are colored by the pair of axes that spans them, the same grammar the bands use in the plane.

And the plane has most of this ladder already, built before any of it was read: §5.0's singularity solids are π(C3), π(C4) and π(C5) — the hexagon, the octagon and the decagon — and a Penrose tile is π(C2). What the pentagrid cannot reach is k = 6: that needs a sixth zone, a family of lines with no direction, which the plane has nowhere to put. The icosahedral hexagrid has it, and the rest of this section is about what it builds.
